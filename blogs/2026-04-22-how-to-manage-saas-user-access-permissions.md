<p>To reduce SaaS stack security risk, maintain effective governance and remain compliant, IT needs to effectively <a href="/monitor/what-is-saas-management/">manage the SaaS stack</a> and the SaaS user access permissions that come with it.</p>



<p>Specifically, IT must actively control what users access which SaaS apps, as well as the files, groups, and data within them. Yet for most IT teams, it’s easier said than done.</p>



<p>Why is this the case, and what should IT do about it?</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What is SaaS user access management?</h2>



<p>SaaS user access management is the process of controlling which users can access specific applications, data, and permissions across a SaaS environment to reduce security risk and maintain compliance.</p>



<p>It ensures that employees only have the access they need—no more, no less—while enabling IT teams to monitor, enforce, and audit permissions across all SaaS applications.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-what-makes-managing-saas-user-access-difficult">What makes managing SaaS user access difficult</h2>



<p>For starters, terminology and access levels are different in each app. Google Workspace has different access levels and permissions than Canva, which is different from Workday, and so on.</p>



<p>Some grant user permissions for specific tasks or projects, while others do not. While some are much better than others, many SaaS apps offer little in the way of <a href="/monitor/polp-vs-rbac/">granular access roles</a>.</p>



<p>And depending on a user’s task at hand, all too often, access levels must increase—forcing IT to assign super admin permissions, simply because it’s the only option.</p>



<p>This is when SaaS user access permissions start to creep.</p>



<p>Forgotten long after that task is done, that super admin status lives on. And over time, excess privileges proliferate unexamined throughout your SaaS stack.</p>



<p>Lastly, IT often lacks a global view of all these super admins across all apps, unable to manage what they can’t see. Before you know it, that ballooning super admin sprawl gives rise to <a href="https://www.verizon.com/business/resources/reports/dbir/">security risks like cred</a><a href="https://www.verizon.com/business/resources/reports/dbir/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">e</a><a href="https://www.verizon.com/business/resources/reports/dbir/">ntial theft</a>, jeopardizing SaaS governance and compliance success.</p>



<p>Without the proper policies, processes, and technology in place, it’s nearly impossible to unravel the sprawl to effectively manage SaaS user access permissions. But with it, IT can confront the challenge.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading">What risks come from excessive SaaS permissions?</h3>



<p>Excessive SaaS permissions increase the risk of:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Credential theft and account compromise</li>



<li>Insider threats (malicious or accidental)</li>



<li>Data exposure through improper sharing</li>



<li>Compliance violations and audit failures</li>
</ul>



<p>When IT teams cannot see or control access centrally, these risks multiply across the SaaS stack.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-best-practices-for-managing-saas-user-access-permissions">Best practices for managing SaaS user access permissions</h2>



<p>Enforcing consistent, granular SaaS user access permissions policy does three important things for your SaaS stack:</p>



<p><strong>1.</strong> I<strong>mproves SaaS security.</strong> It reduces risks related to stolen credentials and insider threats, should they be negligent or malicious, as well as improper file sharing or misconfigured settings.</p>



<p><strong>2. Makes SaaS governance and compliance programs more effective</strong>. In <a href="/what-is-saas-governance/">SaaS governance</a> and compliance, there’s a concept known as “design effectiveness.” Extending beyond the binary “Yes or no, does this process comply?” question, it measures <em>how well</em> you meet goals.</p>



<p>Granular policies and controls around SaaS user access permissions —that are set by role, department, or title—allow your IT team to improve how well you comply, thereby making it more effective.</p>



<p><strong>3. Meets requirements for higher levels of compliance certifications.</strong> For example, higher effectiveness may help comply with Level 2 requirements, instead of just Level 1.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-first-define-saas-app-user-access-privileges">First define SaaS app user access privileges</h3>



<p>Before you can enforce your granular security policy, you first need to <a href="/monitor/the-fundamentals-of-role-based-access-control/">define roles across your organization</a>, and it needs a reliable source of truth. This is typically in an HRIS that connects to a <a href="/monitor/best-practices-for-saas-management/">SaaS management platform</a> like BetterCloud to help enforce that policy.</p>



<p>Next <a href="https://support.bettercloud.com/hc/en-us/articles/115003089288-Role-Based-Privileges" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">set roles and SaaS user access</a> permissions. Don’t forget to do both users and super admin roles.</p>



<p>There are <a href="/what-are-the-4-types-of-access-control/">four main types of access control models</a> (role-based, attribute-based, discretionary, and mandatory) that organizations use to manage how users are granted access to SaaS applications.</p>



<p>If you’re aiming for effective SaaS governance and compliance, you should make sure to adhere to<a href="/what-is-privileged-access-management/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank"> least privilege access</a>.</p>



<p>This proven security design principle states that a user should only have access to the specific SaaS apps and data needed to complete a task. Organizations that practice least privilege access greatly improve their security posture by reducing many risks.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-next-enforce-policy-on-those-permissions">Next enforce policy on those permissions</h3>



<p><strong>1. Grant least privilege user access for all apps.</strong> Remember that SaaS user access privileges should be set as low as possible. Higher-level permissions should be granted temporarily as needed.</p>



<p>Your IT team should consider the following actions:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Using your SaaS apps’ built-in access rules or admin privileges to keep user permissions, make sure you separate standard accounts from admin accounts, and higher-level system functions from lower ones. For all organizations, it’s a healthy practice to have super admins perform admin-related tasks in a dedicated super admin account, and user-related tasks in its own separate user account.</li>
</ul>



<p>As an example: An IT team member should perform Google Workspace super admin tasks in a super admin account and use a user account for other work-related tasks.</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Create a SaaS user app access change request process that uses a ticketing system like an ITSM or Google Forms. This way, it’ll be easier to <a href="https://www.bettercloud.com/purpose-of-user-access-review/">review them</a>, compare them to your documented security policy, and reject or change access level requests accordingly.</li>
</ul>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Raise user access permissions to a higher tier for the shortest possible duration. You can do this by using expiring privileges and one-time-use credentials to keep access to only when needed. If higher-level SaaS user access permissions must be granted for a longer time interval, and that user will be departing, make sure you suspend higher level privileges within a few hours departure notification.</li>
</ul>



<p><strong>2. Track SaaS user access permission changes.</strong> It’s important to keep track of user IDs, one-time passwords, offboarded users, and dates of changes, so it reduces risk and easier to prove compliance.</p>



<p><strong>3. Conduct regular audits. </strong>Make it a habit to monitor all super admin and user permissions in all SaaS apps to stop excess user access privileges, and corresponding risks, from piling up in your SaaS stack.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading">How do you manage SaaS user access permissions?</h2>



<p>To manage SaaS user access effectively, IT teams should follow a structured, repeatable process.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Step-by-step process:</h3>



<ol class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Define roles using a source of truth</strong><strong><br /></strong> Use an HRIS or identity provider to establish accurate user roles.</li>



<li><strong>Assign role-based access permissions</strong><strong><br /></strong> Map roles to appropriate access levels across SaaS applications.</li>



<li><strong>Enforce least privilege access</strong><strong><br /></strong> Ensure users only have access necessary for their tasks.</li>



<li><strong>Separate admin and user accounts</strong><strong><br /></strong> Use dedicated accounts for administrative tasks to reduce risk.</li>



<li><strong>Implement access request workflows</strong><strong><br /></strong> Use ITSM tools or forms to review and approve access changes.</li>



<li><strong>Use temporary and expiring permissions</strong><strong><br /></strong> Grant elevated access only for limited durations.</li>



<li><strong>Track all permission changes</strong><strong><br /></strong> Monitor user activity, access changes, and authentication events.</li>



<li><strong>Conduct regular audits</strong><strong><br /></strong> <a href="https://www.bettercloud.com/monitor/guide-to-user-access-reviews/">Review permissions</a> across all SaaS apps to eliminate unnecessary access.&nbsp;&nbsp;</li>
</ol>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What are the benefits of enforcing SaaS access policies?</h2>



<p>Enforcing consistent SaaS access policies provides three major benefits:</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading">1. Improved security</h3>



<p>Reduces risks related to credential theft, insider threats, and misconfigured access.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading">2. Stronger governance and compliance</h3>



<p>Enhances “design effectiveness,” improving how well organizations meet compliance requirements.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading">3. Higher compliance certification readiness</h3>



<p>Supports achieving more advanced compliance levels (e.g., Level 2 vs. Level 1).</p>
